The Windmill Base (1st Floor)

  • A Living Legend: Welcome to the "Mill of the Priest"! You are standing inside the oldest working windmill on the entire island and in the whole Dodecanese complex. It is over 400-years-old, operated daily all the way up until 1987, and is still a living machine that grinds flour today!
  • At the Entrance: As you step through the doorway, notice the traditional pomegranate hanging for good luck, alongside a small bottle of Holy Water from Mount Athos that is over 9-years-old.
  • Built for Storms: This giant machine is 6.2 meters tall, and the stone walls around you are 1.2 meters thick to keep the building safe from wild sea storms.
  • Massive Machinery: Up on the higher floors, there are two massive grinding stones that weigh one ton each that's over two tons together!
  • Ancient Wood: 80% of the wood in this building is original, and the rest was brought all the way from Mount Athos. Amazingly, some of the reused wooden parts spinning above are between 1,000 and 1,300-years-old!
  • To Your Right: See the area covered with a rag? That was the miller's storage room. Next to it, you will see a wooden donkey saddle and a threshing sledge that the miller's donkey would pull to separate the wheat. You can also spot everyday wooden tools and beautiful photos of other windmills across Greece.
  • The Fireplace: The miller didn't just use this for warmth! Look closely inside—do you see a can? That holds cow fat, which the miller melted to lubricate the moving parts of the windmill so they wouldn't squeak. Just above the fireplace is a photograph of Kostas Politis, the beloved owner of this windmill whose legacy lives on right here.
  • To Your Left: You will see a traditional scale and weights, which were used to carefully weigh the fresh flour.
  • The First Brakes: Look for the tiny rope hanging down. Believe it or not, that rope was the windmill's first braking system! The miller would pull it to lift those two-ton millstones just a tiny bit, making it safe for him to go outside and unwrap the sails.

The Windmill Machinery (3rd Floor)

  • Mind Your Head: Welcome to the third floor! Please watch your step on the ladder and mind your head as you explore.
  • The Muscle of the Mill: You are now looking at the real power of the windmill. Look at that massive wooden beam above the stones. It is the main connector linking the heavy stones inside, the gears, and the giant spinning sails outside!
  • Two-Ton Stones: These massive grinding stones weigh over two tons together! They were imported all the way from Nisyros Island, which is famous for producing a unique, super-strong stone perfect for milling.
  • The Feeder & The Bell: Look just above the stones for a wooden box called The Feeder, where the miller poured the raw wheat. But how did he know when it was empty over all the loud noise? Look closely for a tiny bell! It would ring automatically when the wheat ran low to tell the miller to refill it.
  • The First Brake: See that wooden lever connected to a tiny rope? That connects all the way down to the first floor and is the first braking system we talked about downstairs.
  • Dropping the Flour: Look down near the lever to find a trap door connecting to a pipe. As soon as the flour was perfectly crushed, it dropped straight down that pipe to the bottom floor!
  • The Miller's "Tip": Right next to the main pipe is a second, smaller trap door. In the old days, people didn't always pay with money. Instead, the miller used this door to collect his "tip" keeping a small percentage of the flour for his own family as payment for his hard work!
  • Smooth Spinning: Spot the little can near the trap doors. Downstairs he used cow fat, but up here, he used a special mixture of olive oil and soap to keep the upper gears running smoothly.
  • The Emergency Brake: Look next to the ladder for a really thick rope. If the miller needed to completely stop making flour in a hurry, he pulled this rope the second braking system!
  • A Ship's Ceiling: Lastly, look up at the ceiling. What does it remind you of? It is actually designed in the shape of a ship to enhance the power of the mechanism and produce flour more quickly!

The Traditional House

  • A Tiny, Crowded Home: Welcome to the traditional house! This amazing little home is over 130 years old. It measures less than 20 square meters, but believe it or not, 8 to 10 people used to live here! The grandparents, the parents, and all of their children shared this one single room.
  • Where Did Everyone Sleep? The grandparents slept right over by the warm fireplace. The parents slept up high in the loft bed on the opposite side, while the older children slept in the beds next to it and on the couch.
  • Rock-a-Bye Baby: What about the baby? Look for the little wooden cradle! To help the baby fall asleep, someone could just gently pull that tiny hanging rope to rock it.
  • Clever Storage: Notice the space underneath the family beds that was their main storage room.
  • Daily Chores & Decor: Over on the left side, you can see a large wooden loom they used to make new clothes and repair old ones, alongside a chest where they kept their personal items. You’ll also spot a sifter used for flour, and beautiful old photographs and religious pictures on the walls.
  • Vases & Uniforms: Next to the chest, look for two vases: the large one was for wine, and the small one was for water. Just above them hang two beautiful traditional uniforms that the ladies of the house wore for weddings and special events.
  • The Heart of the Home: Since the house wasn't restored like modern homes, the fireplace was used to cook all their meals and keep the whole family warm. Right next to it is a small hand-grinder mill for grinding flour, along with their daily kitchenware and traditional plates decorated with pretty painted flowers.
  • The Hanging Swing: Look up at the ceiling! Do you see something hanging that looks a bit like a swing? It’s not for playing! They kept their fresh bread in the top part to keep it safely away from hungry mice. In the bottom part, they stored carobs. Back then, they didn't have candy bars, so sweet carobs were given to the children as a delicious chocolate treat!
  • A Traditional Refrigerator: Lastly, look for the wooden cabinet. Believe it or not, this is a traditional refrigerator! It has two parts. They put big ice cubes in the top, and as the ice melted, they could open a little tap to drink the ice-cold water. The cold from the top part also chilled the bottom section perfectly to keep their food fresh!

Welcome to the Stone Oven! This beautiful stone oven is over 150 years old. Back in the day, the majority of the ladies in the village would come right here to bake their daily bread for their families.

Baking bread back then wasn't as fast as just running to the supermarket! It was actually a two-day process. The night before, the ladies would carefully mix and shape the dough, leaving it to rest and rise up overnight. The next morning, when the dough was done, they would build a hot fire to warm up the oven. Once the temperature was just right, they placed the dough inside. After baking for one to two hours, the entire village would be filled with the delicious smell of fresh, warm bread ready to be eaten!

If you look around, you can see some wonderful pictures on display that show exactly how they did this whole procedure.
